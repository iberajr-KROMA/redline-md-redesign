Contract terms, explained

Tail coverage

Many malpractice policies are claims-made: they only cover claims filed while the policy is active. When you leave a job, you need tail coverage (an extended reporting endorsement) or the next employer's policy must include prior acts coverage. Who pays for it is often set in your contract, and the cost can reach $10,000 or more.

Claims-made vs. occurrence

An occurrence policy covers incidents that happen during the policy period, no matter when the claim is filed, so it needs no tail. A claims-made policy covers claims filed while it is active, so leaving requires tail or prior acts coverage. Ask which one your employer carries.

Non-compete clauses

A non-compete limits where and for how long you can practice after you leave. Check the radius, the length of time and which facilities it covers. Enforceability varies by state, so a clause that looks harsh may or may not hold up.

Termination without cause

Many contracts let either side end the agreement without cause after a notice period. Check how much notice you must give, what happens to pay and benefits during that time, and who pays for tail coverage when you go.

Sign-on bonus repayment

Sign-on bonuses, relocation payments and loan repayment help are often repayable if you leave early. Look for the repayment schedule, what triggers it and whether it is pro-rated.

What is tail insurance and why does it matter?

Tail insurance, also called extended reporting coverage, protects you from malpractice claims filed after a claims-made policy ends. If your contract makes you pay for it when you leave, the cost can reach $10,000 or more. Checking who pays before you sign lets you negotiate it.

The redline marks clauses that put you at risk, such as tail coverage, non-compete terms and termination language, and shows proposed changes you can bring to your employer.
